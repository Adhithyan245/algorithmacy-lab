## What it argues

Leonardi and Leavell study experts who use AI tools that generate highly detailed, realistic representations, and they name what those representations can produce: **"artificial certainty,"** defined in the abstract as "the illusion that complex future outcomes are definitively knowable, even though they are inherently uncertain." The evidence is a comparative study of two urban planning organizations using the same AI simulation tool. Three representational practices shape how laypeople perceive a representation's certainty — controlling the level of detail, shaping stakeholder engagement, and constructing the model's meaning. Where process experts practise *enhancement*, amplifying the technology's capabilities, stakeholders mistake representations for reality and expert authority erodes; where they practise *modulation*, tempering how outputs are presented, they keep the authority needed "to keep uncertainty alive." The authors close on a distinction between representations *of* the future and representations *for* the future.

## Relation to the argument

**Supplies the introduction's claim that AI produces confidence it has not earned, but its certainty is about the future and its victims are an audience.** The author's introduction cites it for algorithmic black boxes "paradoxically manufacturing a false sense of epistemological security". "Artificial certainty" is the paper's term and "illusion" is in its definition, so the gist holds. Three differences should be visible in the draft's wording. The illusion concerns *future outcomes*, not knowledge in general. It arises in *laypeople and stakeholders* looking at representations that experts made and showed them, not in the person operating the system. And it arises from how experts *present* outputs — enhancement against modulation — so the paper's cause is a practice, not opacity; the abstract never calls the tool a black box. "Paradoxically" and "epistemological security" are the author's words.

The study has a triadic shape the draft could use more directly than it does: an expert, a lay stakeholder, and an AI representation standing between them, with the expert's handling of the representation deciding what the stakeholder believes. That is closer to the paper's own triad than the sentence it is currently cited for.
