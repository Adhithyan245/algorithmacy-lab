# C. Probability communication, display and decision support

Facet C of the Decision Experience Design (DXD) Part 3 literature review (decision markets such as Polymarket and Kalshi as decision experience design). Agent-drafted on 2026-10-03. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text below is the author's.

What this facet covers. How people read a price or an odds quote as a probability; what the risk-communication literature says about number format, numeracy and display; how probability words and forecast graphics are understood; and whether a probability that comes from a crowd, a market or an algorithm moves decisions in calibrated ways. The synthesis answers three questions. C1: how people read prices as probabilities and what format effects the risk-communication literature documents. C2: what research says about displaying probabilities to support decisions. C3: whether exposure to a crowd or market probability changes decisions in calibrated ways.

## Entries

### C1. Reading prices and odds as probabilities, and format effects

gigerenzer1995frequency. Gigerenzer et al. (1995), Psychological Review, doi 10.1037/0033-295x.102.4.684. Abstract only. The abstract argues that Bayesian algorithms are computationally simpler in frequency formats than in the probability formats used in earlier work, and that statistically naive participants derived up to 50% of inferences by Bayesian algorithms when information came in frequency formats. Bearing: supports. This is the base result for the claim that format, not the mind, produces many probability errors. Whether a price quoted in cents is closer to a probability format or a frequency format is not addressed here.

mcdowell2017meta. McDowell et al. (2017), Psychological Bulletin, doi 10.1037/bul0000126. Abstract only. A meta-analysis of 35 articles and 226 performance estimates. The abstract reports that short menu formats and visual aids were among the strongest moderators and improved performance in both probability and natural-frequency formats, and that numeracy and expertise were tested as moderators. A later paper (weber2018frequency) cites McDowell and Jacobs (2017) as showing that natural frequencies raised performance to about 24% from about 4%; I did not see that figure in the McDowell abstract, so it rests on the Weber abstract. Bearing: supports, with a size warning. Format helps, and most people still fail.

chapman2009numeracy. Chapman et al. (2009), Judgment and Decision Making, doi 10.1017/s1930297500000681. Abstract only. In 342 students the natural-frequency benefit "occurred primarily for participants who scored high in numeracy." Bearing: complicates. Format is not a numeracy-free fix, which matters for the numeracy model in Part 1: format effects and numeracy interact.

siegrist2011natural, weber2018frequency, sirota2024wording, stegmuller2024joint. Four abstracts that qualify the frequency effect. Siegrist et al. (2011, doi 10.1080/13669877.2011.571786) find that in general-population samples natural frequencies beat probabilities, but a majority still fail, and a social problem is easier than a medical one. Weber et al. (2018, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01833) find that many participants given frequencies translated them back into probabilities. Sirota et al. (2024, doi 10.1016/j.cognition.2024.105722; N = 4909 in seven experiments) find the frequency advantage appears "only with the improved wording." Stegmuller et al. (2024, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1296359) find no frequency advantage for joint probabilities and a format by visualization interaction. All abstract only. Bearing: complicates. The format literature supports a design claim about how a price should be shown only for one task, Bayesian updating from conditional information. A single contract price is a different task.

reyna2009numeracy. Reyna et al. (2009), Psychological Bulletin, doi 10.1037/a0017327. Abstract only. Reviews health numeracy; low numeracy "increases susceptibility to effects of ... how information is presented (e.g., as frequencies vs. percentages)" and to framing and ratio bias; outlines psychophysical, computational, dual-process and fuzzy-trace accounts. Bearing: supports. It ties numeracy to format susceptibility directly, which is the mechanism the Part 3 argument needs.

reyna2023numeracy. Reyna and Brainerd (2023), Nature Reviews Psychology, doi 10.1038/s44159-023-00188-7. Reused from F7, where it was read in full (Europe PMC). F7 records that skilled numeracy is gist, not verbatim computation. Bearing: supports. A price of 63 cents read verbatim is a number; read as gist it is "more likely than not." The parallel to markets is this review's inference, not the authors' claim.

peters2006numeracy. Peters et al. (2006), Psychological Science, doi 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01720.x. Reused from F7 (abstract only, closed). Not returned by the Consensus query in row 14 and not re-matched. Carries the independence-from-intelligence claim already used in Part 1.

peters2008dissecting, peters2012beyond, peters2013numbers. Peters and colleagues. Abstract only for all three. Peters et al. (2008, doi 10.1017/s1930297500000012): in five Asian Disease variants the choices of the less numerate showed a large frame effect beyond their evaluations of the separate options, while the choices of the highly numerate were "almost completely accounted for" by those evaluations. Peters (2012, doi 10.1177/0963721411429960): greater numeracy relates to reduced susceptibility to framing, less influence of nonnumerical information such as mood, and greater sensitivity to numerical risk levels. Peters et al. (2013, doi 10.1177/0272989x13511705; N = 905): numeric adverse-event information raised risk comprehension across numeracy and age levels relative to nonnumeric information, with risk overestimation falling from 69% to 18% among the less numerate. Bearing: supports for the independent-moderator claim, and a second use: numbers can help the less numerate when the alternative is vague words. A market's number is not the vague alternative.

galesic2010statistical, garciaretamero2009communicating, garciaretamero2017visual. Galesic and Garcia-Retamero. Abstract only. The 2010 survey (doi 10.1001/archinternmed.2009.481) finds objective numeracy of 68.5% correct items in Germany and 64.5% in the United States, a large education gap, and subjective numeracy as a good indicator of objective numeracy. The 2009 study (doi 10.2105/ajph.2009.160234) finds denominator neglect, strongest among the less numerate, removed by icon arrays, and concludes that "Problems understanding numerical information often reside not in the mind but in the problem's representation." The 2017 review (doi 10.1177/0018720817690634; 36 publications, 27,885 participants) finds transparent visual aids robustly improve risk understanding, most for less skilled people. Bearing: supports. Numeracy is measurable at population scale, and display can offset low numeracy. Both points cut toward the design argument.

fagerlin2007making, fagerlin2011helping. Fagerlin and colleagues. Abstract only. Fagerlin et al. (2007, doi 10.5555/ajhb.2007.31.supp.s47) conclude that how risks are presented "can significantly affect both patients' risk perceptions and their knowledge" and that methods may need to differ for high and low numeracy. Fagerlin et al. (2011, doi 10.1093/jnci/djr318) list ten empirically supported methods, among them frequencies over relative risks, absolute risks, pictographs and plain language. Bearing: supports. These are the design baselines against which a contract page can be judged.

sirota2018oneinx, bonner2021fundamental, trevena2021advanced, ancker2025point, keller2009format, millroth2015denominator, lipkus2007formats. Format-effect evidence. Abstract only for all. Sirota et al. (2018, doi 10.1037/xap0000190; N = 975): "1-in-X" ratios produced higher probability perceptions than equivalent "N-in-X*N" ratios and affected decisions. Bonner et al. (2021, doi 10.1177/0272989x21996328) recommend a clear denominator over a set time period, consistent formats across options, and interpreting numbers for readers of varying numeracy. Trevena et al. (2021, doi 10.1177/0272989x21996342) add that simple visual formats help but "can be misleading if not well designed" and that graph literacy moderates them. Ancker et al. (2025, doi 10.1177/23814683241255337) find moderate to strong evidence that format changes probability perceptions and feelings but that link to behavior is weaker. Keller et al. (2009, doi 10.1177/0272989x09333122): format effects on risk perception depend on numeracy. Millroth et al. (2015, doi 10.1016/j.cognition.2015.01.014): numeracy interacts with probability denominator in pricing prospects. Lipkus (2007, doi 10.1177/0272989x07307271): few firm best-practice claims across numeric, verbal and visual formats. Bearing: supports and complicates. Format effects on perception are well documented. Format effects on behavior are less so, and that limits any claim that a contract's display changes what people do.

andersson2015bettors. Andersson et al. (2015), Journal of Behavioral Decision Making, doi 10.1002/bdm.1851. Abstract only. Three web surveys with 186 experienced bettors converting odds to frequency judgments: the bettors were "well-calibrated," but "unable to consciously adjust judgments for different margins." Bearing: supports, and it is the closest evidence on how odds users handle the overround. The population is experienced sports bettors, not Polymarket users.

nilsson2011odds. Consensus returns a conference-style abstract (Nilsson et al., 2011, "Are bets with the odds of 3.1 more likely than bets with the odds of 21/10?") with no DOI and no venue. It reports that punters underestimated implied probabilities for decimal odds and overestimated them for fractional odds (mean error -3.69 vs 4.49). It is not in the bib. See the [verify] list.

strumbelj2014odds, clarke2017overround, hegarty2025loss. How a vig is removed. Abstract only for all. Strumbelj (2014, doi 10.1016/j.ijforecast.2014.02.008): probabilities from Shin's model are more accurate than from basic normalization or regression. Clarke et al. (2017, doi 10.11648/j.ajss.20170506.12): normalization, additive and Shin methods each have defects, and a power method does better. Hegarty et al. (2025, doi 10.1080/00036846.2025.2507979): if bookmakers load margin on longshots, the equal-weighted average loss rate exceeds what the overround predicts. Bearing: supports. Even among specialists, the step from quoted price to fair probability has competing methods. None of these papers studies a lay reader.

wolfers2004prediction, manski2006interpreting, nam2026calibration. Wolfers et al. (2004, doi 10.1257/0895330041371321): market-generated forecasts are typically fairly accurate and outperform moderately sophisticated benchmarks. Manski (2006; NBER doi 10.3386/w10359): with risk-neutral traders with heterogeneous beliefs, the price "reveals nothing about the dispersion of traders' beliefs and partially identifies the central tendency," so "market probability" is not an identified quantity. Le (2026, arXiv preprint, doi 10.48550/arxiv.2602.19520): across 353 million trades and 429,000 binary contracts on Kalshi and Polymarket, the most robust pattern is underconfidence in political markets, and "a price's meaning depends on what, when and how much is traded." All abstract only. Bearing: supports. The price is a quantity whose probabilistic meaning is conditional. A display that calls it "chance" asserts more than the theory or the data license. The Wolfers (2006) paper "Interpreting Prediction Market Prices as Probabilities" appears in Consensus as several records with no abstract and no DOI, so it is in the [verify] list.

### C2. Displaying probabilities to support decisions

padilla2018decision, spiegelhalter2011visualizing, spiegelhalter2017risk, hullman2019pursuit, hullman2019why. Overviews. Abstract only. Padilla et al. (2018, doi 10.1186/s41235-018-0120-9) propose an integrative dual-process model of decision making with visualizations. Spiegelhalter et al. (2011, doi 10.1126/science.1191181): "limited experimental evidence on how different types of visualizations are processed," with effectiveness depending on audience numeracy. Spiegelhalter (2017, doi 10.1146/annurev-statistics-010814-020148) reviews numbers, verbal expressions and graphics, with gambling among the case studies. Hullman et al. (2019, doi 10.1109/tvcg.2018.2864889) survey 86 uncertainty-visualization studies and find a bias toward evaluating accuracy over decision quality. Hullman (2019, doi 10.1109/tvcg.2019.2934287): authors who agree uncertainty is valuable still usually omit it. Bearing: supports. Omitting uncertainty is the default, which is the design fact about a market page that shows a bare percentage.

fernandes2018quantile, kale2020effectsize, padilla2021uncertain, matzen2023numerical. Display experiments. Abstract only. Fernandes et al. (2018, doi 10.1145/3173574.3173718): quantile dotplots with 50 outcomes gave expected payoffs 97% of optimal, 5 points above control, and beat textual uncertainty. Kale et al. (2020, doi 10.1109/tvcg.2020.3030335): adding means to uncertainty displays biased magnitude estimates and decisions, and "visualization designs that support the least biased effect size estimation do not support the best decision-making." Padilla et al. (2021, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2020.579267): people used a forecaster's stated confidence as well as the dotplot and became more conservative when confidence was lower. Matzen et al. (2023, doi 10.1109/mcg.2023.3299875): icon arrays and natural frequencies produced different evacuation rates, so "design choices are not neutral." Bearing: supports. Displays change decisions, not only comprehension, and a display of a single point (a price) is the type the first two studies warn about.

gigerenzer2005rain, joslyn2009pop, juanchich2016pop, juanchich2018gloomy, ripberger2022living. Probability of precipitation. Abstract only. Gigerenzer et al. (2005, doi 10.1111/j.1539-6924.2005.00608.x): in five cities only in New York did most pedestrians give the standard meteorological reading, and the stated cause is a missing reference class. Joslyn et al. (2009, doi 10.1175/2008bams2509.1): many students read the percentage as proportion of area or time, and performance improved only when the probability of no precipitation was also given. Juanchich et al. (2016, doi 10.1080/13669877.2014.983945; N = 1337): most UK and US respondents misread it, a stated reference class helped only when the target area was explicit, and a verbal format did not fix it. Juanchich et al. (2018, doi 10.1080/20445911.2018.1553884): misreadings are partly a fluency artifact of how the response options are worded, giving "a more optimistic picture." Ripberger et al. (2022, doi 10.1175/wcas-d-21-0034.1): a living systematic review concluding that average people can use probability information if presentation is considered and that, given appropriate presentation, it generally improves decision quality. Bearing: complicates and supports. A single-event percentage is ambiguous without a reference class. A contract price carries a reference class only by the contract's resolution wording, which the price display often hides. The 2018 and 2022 papers also warn against overstating public misunderstanding.

beythmarom1982probable, wintle2019verbal, dhami2022words. Probability words. Abstract only. Beyth-Marom (1982, doi 10.1002/for.3980010305): high variability in numeric translation of verbal expressions, higher in context. Wintle et al. (2019, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0213522; N = 924): correspondence with a published numeric guideline was 66% when numbers were bracketed in the text and 32% without guidelines. Dhami et al. (2022, doi 10.1016/j.tics.2022.03.002): senders prefer words, but "when making consequential decisions, recipients prefer (precise) numeric probabilities." Bearing: complicates. The words-versus-numbers literature favors numbers for consequential decisions, which tells against a claim that a market's numeric price is the problem. The case against the display has to rest on what the number leaves out, not on its being a number.

### C3. Crowd, market and algorithmic advice

dietvorst2015aversion, logg2019appreciation. Dietvorst et al. (2015, doi 10.1037/xge0000033): after seeing an algorithm err, people lose confidence faster than in a human and choose it less, even when it outperforms. Logg et al. (2019, doi 10.1016/j.obhdp.2018.12.005): lay people "adhere more to advice when they think it comes from an algorithm than from a person," and professionals with forecasting expertise relied less on it, which "hurt their accuracy." Abstract only. Bearing: complicates. The two results point in opposite directions and depend on conditions, so exposure to a machine-aggregated probability has no single predicted effect.

burton2019review, jussupow2024integrative, hou2021expert. Reconciliations. Abstract only. Burton et al. (2019, doi 10.1002/bdm.2155) review 61 articles and sort causes into expectations and expertise, decision autonomy, incentivization, cognitive compatibility and divergent rationalities. Jussupow et al. (2024, doi 10.25300/misq/2024/18512) argue the constructs lack a shared definition and measurement. Hou et al. (2021, doi 10.1145/3479864) produce both aversion and appreciation by changing only the description of the human and the algorithm, mediated by perceived competence. Bearing: complicates. Framing of the source, which a platform controls, can flip the effect.

yaniv2000advice, yaniv2012blindfold, soll2020badthing, loehre2024gameshow, milyavsky2024framing. Advice taking. Abstract only. Yaniv et al. (2000, doi 10.1006/obhd.2000.2909): advice is discounted relative to one's own opinion, and advisor reputation forms fast and is revised asymmetrically. Yaniv (2012, doi 10.1002/bdm.740): participants who could not form a prior opinion weighted others' opinions better and were more accurate. Soll et al. (2020, SSRN doi 10.2139/ssrn.3094176): agreeing advice leaves point estimates unchanged and raises confidence, which can worsen overconfidence. Lohre et al. (2024, doi 10.1017/jdm.2024.4): contestants on a high-stakes game show gave an average weight of advice of 0.58, above the usual egocentric discounting. Milyavsky et al. (2024, doi 10.1016/j.obhdp.2024.104328; N = 3,411): the same estimates got more weight when framed as advice than as opinions. Bearing: complicates. Calibrated use is the exception: advice can be underweighted, overweighted or confidence-inflating. Whether a visible market price acts as advice, opinion or fact is a framing choice.

schultze2022crowd, fiedler2019myopia, morin2020waste. Crowd information as input. Abstract only. Schultze et al. (2022, doi 10.1037/dec0000194): people showed no sign of using the size of the crowd but did use the distribution of its judgments, and those given crowd estimates "might underestimate the variance of the underlying individual judgments." Fiedler et al. (2019, doi 10.1002/bdm.2109): people were "strongly affected by any advice" about health risks, including invalid advice, independent of numeracy. Morin et al. (2020, doi 10.1098/rstb.2020.0052): a review of 45 studies showing social information waste and four showing over-use. Bearing: supports. A displayed market probability conveys neither crowd size nor dispersion, and the one study here that tests numeracy as a moderator of overuse (Fiedler) finds no moderation.

frey2020sequential, becker2017network, winkler2019combination. Aggregation and display. Abstract only. Frey et al. (2020, doi 10.1287/mnsc.2020.3713): showing earlier votes can make majorities wrong more often because early errors cascade. Becker et al. (2017, doi 10.1073/pnas.1615978114): social influence can improve group accuracy in decentralized networks but raises error in centralized ones. Winkler et al. (2019, doi 10.1287/deca.2019.0391): the vision of increased exposure to improved visualizations of probability forecasts improving public understanding. Bearing: complicates. A live price that traders and readers both see is a feedback display. These studies say it can help or hurt depending on structure, and none tests a lay audience reading a continuously updating price.

## Synthesis

C1. The literature on how people read a price as a probability is thin in exactly the place the essay needs. Nothing returned tests how lay users move from cents on the dollar to a chance, or how a contract's resolution wording and fees enter that reading. What exists is adjacent. Odds-to-probability reading by experienced sports bettors is mostly calibrated but does not adjust for the margin (Andersson et al., 2015). Methods for stripping the overround from odds compete and are tested on forecasters, not lay users (Strumbelj, 2014; Clarke et al., 2017). The economics literature treats the price as a probability under conditions that heterogeneous beliefs break (Manski, 2006), and a 2026 preprint finds Kalshi and Polymarket prices underconfident in political markets, so the price's meaning is conditional on domain, timing and trade size (Le, 2026). The risk-communication literature supplies the format and numeracy mechanism. Natural frequencies, icon arrays and absolute risks help, the help is moderated by numeracy and wording, and most people still fail Bayesian tasks even in frequency format (Gigerenzer et al., 1995; McDowell et al., 2017; Chapman et al., 2009; Sirota et al., 2024; Reyna et al., 2009). Numeracy predicts less framing susceptibility and more use of numbers (Peters, 2012). So the supported claim is that format sensitivity is a function of numeracy, and the Part 3 inference that a price display is a format choice with numeracy-dependent effects is the essay's to make.

C2. The display literature says a bare point estimate is the weak design. Uncertainty displays improve decisions in incentivized tasks (Fernandes et al., 2018), displays change decisions and not only comprehension (Matzen et al., 2023; Kale et al., 2020), and authors who value uncertainty routinely omit it (Hullman, 2019). Forecast communication supplies the closest analog. Probability of precipitation is misread when the reference class is missing (Gigerenzer et al., 2005; Joslyn et al., 2009), and the fix is to state the event, including its complement. A contract price has the same exposure: its reference class is the resolution rule. Two cautions keep the claim modest. Later work finds public misreading of precipitation probabilities overstated in part by answer wording (Juanchich et al., 2018), and the living review finds people can use probabilities when well presented (Ripberger et al., 2022). The words-versus-numbers literature favors numbers for consequential choices (Dhami et al., 2022), so the objection to a market display cannot be that it uses a number.

C3. No study returned tests whether a displayed crowd or market probability changes lay decisions in calibrated ways. The nearest evidence points to conditionality. Algorithm aversion and appreciation both occur and can be produced by changing the source's description (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Logg et al., 2019; Hou et al., 2021). Advice is discounted, over-weighted or confidence-inflating depending on framing and agreement (Yaniv et al., 2000; Soll et al., 2020; Milyavsky et al., 2024). Social information is used without regard to crowd size and with weak attention to dispersion (Schultze et al., 2022), and a visible tally can cascade early errors (Frey et al., 2020). The defensible statement is that calibration of response to a crowd probability is not guaranteed and depends on display choices a platform makes.

## Gaps

- No empirical study found of how lay users read a decision-market price (cents on the dollar) as a probability, or of the effect of resolution wording, fees or the bid-ask spread on that reading. This is the central gap for Part 3 and is not closed by adjacent literatures.
- No study found of a market or crowd probability displayed to lay decision makers and its effect on calibration of their decisions.
- Numeracy as a moderator of crowd-probability use: only Fiedler et al. (2019) tests it, and finds none. The numeracy model for algorithmacy needs a market-specific test.
- Every entry is abstract only. Claims about sample sizes and effect directions are as stated in abstracts. Quotations are from abstracts.
- Crossref and OpenAlex were blocked, so DOIs are matched to Consensus records only. Author lists are truncated.
