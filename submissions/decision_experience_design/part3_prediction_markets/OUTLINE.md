## (a) Thesis

Prediction markets exist to produce a probability. The person who wants that probability must still open a
trading interface, read a price, and place a bet. The essay argues that this gap between what the market
computes and what the interface asks of the user is a decision experience design failure, and that Part 1's
and Part 2's criteria locate it: the interface requires a trading skill the decision does not need, gives the
user no scaffold toward the skill the decision does need, and borrows the form of a betting product.

The altitude follows the repo rule. The essay is modest about what is shown (no study measures how lay users
read Polymarket or Kalshi prices) and confident about the design claim.

## (b) Author ruling (2026-10-03): the target is sports betting dressed as decision markets

The author settled the fork. Kalshi and Polymarket present themselves as prediction and decision markets, and
the product is sports betting in that dress. The design failure is the dress: the decision-market frame
(information, forecasting, a price that guides a choice) licenses an interface built for betting, and the user
who wants a probability gets a bet slip.

## (c) Section plan

1. The promise. Markets as forecasting by aggregation [abstract, Facet A1]. Why a price is only sometimes a
   probability: Manski gives an interval around mean belief, Gjerstad says near the mean under plausible risk
   aversion, and fees, horizon and thin markets break the equality [abstract; Manski and Malone DOIs flagged as
   suspect by the agent]. Backward hook to Part 1: the unit of design is the decision, not the screen.
2. What the interface asks. A trade, a position size, fee and spread exposure, where the decision-maker needs
   a probability and an action [abstract; the agent marks this an inference from abstracts, Facet A3]. D1 (price
   display, order types, defaults, notifications) is empty and must be filled from the platforms' own pages
   [none until fetched].
3. Reading a price. Cents on the dollar as a probability. Format and numeracy interact (Gigerenzer and
   Hoffrage 1995; McDowell 2017; Reyna 2009; Peters) [abstract, Facet C1]. Bare point estimates are the weak
   display; probability-of-precipitation misreading traces to a missing reference class [abstract, C2]. No study
   covers lay readings of cents-on-the-dollar prices or lay calibration after seeing a market probability
   [none]. State the gap; do not fill it.
4. Whether anyone learns. Plain outcome feedback teaches little, calibration feedback does better, the training
   literature is contested (Hauenstein 2024), and apparent learning on markets is partly attrition (Seru 2010)
   [abstract, Facet B3]. Link to Part 1: the competence is algorithmacy, which the interface does not scaffold.
5. The betting form. Notifications have the only causal support among interface features: Rockloff (2026) report
   23% fewer bets, 39% less spending and 67% fewer harms with opt-out [abstract]. Chapkovski (2024) attributes
   70% of the gamification volume gap to self-selection, and Kalda (2021) rules out nudges as the driver of the
   smartphone effect [abstract]. Dark patterns are documented in shopping (Mathur, Luguri) and only catalogued in
   gambling (McGarrigle 2026) [abstract; Luguri and Mathur journal versions in verify list]. No study covers
   event-contract interfaces, streaks or one-tap trading [none]. This section stays an analogy argument.
6. Using the price in a decision. Corporate markets forecast well (Cowgill et al.), but Choo et al. (2022) find
   in an experiment that managers' trust, not accuracy, governs use [abstract]. Futarchy and decision markets are
   designs with no field use [abstract]. Aversion and appreciation of algorithmic or crowd advice flip with
   framing of the source (Facet C3) [abstract]. Numeracy as a moderator of crowd-advice use was tested once and
   found null (Fiedler et al. 2019) [abstract].
7. Resolution and rules. Contract wording, settlement sources and dispute handling shape what a price means.
   Leads only: Polymarket UMA disputes, a reported 1,150 disputed markets in early 2026, the Kalshi Khamenei
   market with a death clause and a rule written afterward [verify, WebSearch summaries]. Section is empty until
   fetched.
8. What a decision market would look like by the Part 1 criteria. Declared operating envelope for the price
   (what the number is and is not), scaffolding toward steering skill, counter-delegation, structural refusal,
   bounded outputs. Each is a proposition the essay can state and a test can falsify. No new jargon beyond Part 1.
9. Limitations and propositions. Names the gaps above flatly: no field study links a price to a decision and its
   outcome; no study of retail use of either platform; the Kalshi and Polymarket calibration papers (Le; Bürgi et
   al.; Cardozo et al.; Dubach) are working papers or preprints [abstract].

## (d) Evidence that cuts against the thesis

Report these in the essay, not around it.
- Cardozo et al. (Polymarket preprint, abstract only) find no favorite-longshot bias in sports [abstract].
- Dubach (Polymarket microstructure preprint) puts median wash trading at 1% [abstract].
- Le and Bürgi et al. find calibration varies by contract type and a favorite-longshot bias [abstract]. Markets
  may be well calibrated where the interface is poor, which makes the critique one of design, not accuracy.
